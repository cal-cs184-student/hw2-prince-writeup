Overview

In this homework, I implemented several techniques for constructing, modifying, and smoothing geometric models. I used de Casteljau's algorithm to evaluate Bezier curves and surfaces, computed area-weighted vertex normals for smooth shading, and implemented local edge flip and edge split operations on a half-edge mesh. I then combined these mesh operations to implement Loop subdivision, including support for boundary edges. As an extra exploration, I also implemented Root-3 subdivision and compared its face-centered refinement with the edge-centered refinement used by Loop subdivision.

The most important lesson I learned is that modifying a complex data structure requires a systematic approach. A half-edge mesh can initially seem confusing because every local operation affects many related iterators at once. However, the process becomes manageable when I first record the complete local neighborhood, create all required elements, and then update every iterator in a fixed order. Clear naming conventions and consistent rules for assigning next, twin, vertex, edge, and face relationships prevent the implementation from becoming disorganized. Following a deliberate sequence made the code easier to reason about, debug, and verify.

Section I: Bezier Curves and Surfaces

Part 1: Bezier curves with 1D de Casteljau subdivision

Given \(n+1\) control points \(P_0,\ldots,P_n\), de Casteljau's algorithm repeatedly linearly interpolates between every pair of adjacent points. Let \(P_i^{(0)}=P_i\). At recursion level \(r\), the new points are

\[ P_i^{(r)}(t)=(1-t)P_i^{(r-1)}(t)+tP_{i+1}^{(r-1)}(t), \qquad 0\leq t\leq 1. \]

Each level contains one fewer point than the previous level, so after \(n\) levels only one point remains. The point on the Bezier curve evaluated at parameter \(t\) is therefore

\[ B(t)=P_0^{(n)}(t). \]

For my six-control-point curve, \(n=5\), so the final point is \(B(t)=P_0^{(5)}(t)\).

Evaluating the algorithm for values of \(t\) throughout \([0,1]\) traces the entire curve.

In my implementation, evaluateStep loops over adjacent input points and appends (1 - t) * points[i] + t * points[i + 1] to a new vector. It returns this vector, which always has one fewer point. Repeating the step until one point remains evaluates the curve at the selected \(t\); sampling many values of \(t\) draws the complete curve.

Part 2: Bezier surfaces with separable 1D de Casteljau

A Bezier surface is defined by a rectangular grid of 3D control points and two parameters, \(u\) and \(v\), which represent positions along the two parametric directions of the surface. Since a Bezier surface is separable, it can be evaluated by applying the 1D de Casteljau algorithm twice. BezierPatch::evaluateStep(points, t) performs one level of linear interpolation between adjacent 3D points and returns a vector with one fewer point. BezierPatch::evaluate1D(points, t) repeatedly calls evaluateStep until only one point remains, thereby evaluating a 1D Bezier curve at \(t\).

To evaluate a point on the surface, BezierPatch::evaluate(u, v) first calls evaluate1D on every row of the control-point grid using \(u\). This reduces each row to one point and produces a list of intermediate 3D points. It then calls evaluate1D on that list using \(v\), reducing the list to the final point on the Bezier surface. Repeating this process for sampled pairs \((u,v)\in[0,1]^2\) generates the entire surface.

Section II: Triangle Meshes and Half-Edge Data Structure

Part 3: Area-weighted vertex normals

To compute the normal at a vertex, I begin with the vertex's representative halfedge and traverse its entire one-ring neighborhood using h = h->twin()->next(). This moves to the next face incident to the same vertex. Boundary faces do not represent surface triangles, so I skip any halfedge whose face satisfies isBoundary().

For each incident triangle, I let \(p_0\) be the current vertex and obtain \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) from the next two halfedges. I then compute the unnormalized face normal

\[ N_f=(p_1-p_0)\times(p_2-p_0). \]

The magnitude of this cross product is twice the area of the triangle. Therefore, adding these unnormalized normals automatically gives larger triangles proportionally more influence than smaller triangles. After the traversal returns to the starting halfedge, I normalize the accumulated vector:

\[ n_v=\frac{\sum_{f\ni v}N_f} {\left\|\sum_{f\ni v}N_f\right\|}. \]

The consistent halfedge winding keeps all incident face normals oriented in the same direction. Returning n.unit() produces the unit vertex normal used for Phong interpolation across the surrounding triangles.

Applying these area-weighted vertex normals does not change the mesh geometry or add any triangles. It changes only how lighting is evaluated. With flat shading, each triangle uses one face normal, so the boundaries between triangles are clearly visible. With Phong shading, the vertex normals are interpolated across each triangle, producing gradual lighting changes between neighboring faces. As a result, the teapot appears much smoother even though its underlying triangle mesh is unchanged.

Part 4: Edge flip

An edge flip operates on two triangles that share an interior edge. It keeps the same vertices, edges, halfedges, and faces, but replaces the shared diagonal with the other diagonal of the quadrilateral. In my implementation, I immediately return e0 if it is a boundary edge because such an edge does not have two adjacent interior triangles.

For an interior edge, h0 and h1 are the two halfedges of e0. Their starting vertices are v0 and v1. I first save the other four local halfedges, h10, h00, h01, and h11, together with their vertices, edges, and the two faces. The opposite vertices are v00 in f0 and v11 in f1. Saving all of these iterators before rewiring prevents the original neighborhood from being lost when the next iterators are changed.

Before the flip, e0 connects v0 and v1. I reuse h0 and h1 for the flipped edge, changing their starting vertices to v11 and v00, respectively. Therefore, e0 now connects the two opposite vertices v11 and v00. The two face cycles change as follows:

Face Before the flip After the flip Representative
f0 h0 → h10 → h00 → h0
v0 → v1 → v00 		h0 → h00 → h01 → h0
v11 → v00 → v0 		f0->halfedge() = h0
f1 h1 → h01 → h11 → h1
v1 → v0 → v11 		h1 → h11 → h10 → h1
v00 → v11 → v1 		f1->halfedge() = h1

I use setNeighbors(next, twin, vertex, edge, face) to reset all five iterators of the six affected halfedges. The outside twin and edge iterators of h00, h01, h10, and h11 do not change, but their next and face assignments are updated to form the two cycles above. I then set the representative halfedges to v0: h01, v1: h10, v00: h00, and v11: h11, and keep e0->halfedge() = h0.

My main debugging strategy was to record the complete local neighborhood before changing it and then explicitly reassign every iterator in the two triangles. Checking that both three-halfedge cycles closed correctly and that each representative halfedge started at its vertex made it easier to catch incorrect next or face assignments. I also tested consecutive flips in the same area of the teapot to confirm that the resulting mesh remained connected.

Part 5: Edge split

For an interior edge \(e0=(b,c)\), the two incident faces are initially \(f0=(b,c,a)\) and \(f1=(c,b,d)\). The split inserts a new vertex \(m\) at the midpoint of \(b\) and \(c\), divides the original edge into \((b,m)\) and \((m,c)\), and connects \(m\) to the two opposite vertices \(a\) and \(d\). The original two triangles are therefore replaced by four triangles.

Before modifying any iterators, I save the entire local neighborhood in iterators: the six original halfedges h00, h10, h20, h01, h11, and h21; the exterior twins; the surrounding edges; the two faces; and the four vertices. This is important because once a next or twin iterator is changed, traversing the mesh can no longer be used to recover the original neighborhood reliably.

I create one vertex m, three edges mc, ma, and md, six halfedges for those edges, and two new faces f2 and f3. The original e0, h00, and h01 are reused for the edge \((b,m)\). I then rebuild each face as the following closed halfedge cycle. Every halfedge in a row is assigned to that row's face.

Face Vertex cycle next cycle and face assignment Representative
f0 (reused) \(b\rightarrow m\rightarrow a\) h00 → h_ma → h20 → h00; all three use face = f0 f0->halfedge() = h00
f1 (reused) \(m\rightarrow b\rightarrow d\) h01 → h11 → h_dm → h01; all three use face = f1 f1->halfedge() = h01
f2 (new) \(m\rightarrow c\rightarrow a\) h_mc → h10 → h_am → h_mc; all three use face = f2 f2->halfedge() = h_mc
f3 (new) \(c\rightarrow m\rightarrow d\) h_cm → h_md → h21 → h_cm; all three use face = f3 f3->halfedge() = h_cm

After establishing these four cycles, I assign the twin pairs (h00, h01), (h_mc, h_cm), (h_ma, h_am), and (h_md, h_dm), and set every halfedge's vertex, edge, and face iterators with setNeighbors. I set the edge representatives to e0->halfedge() = h00, mc->halfedge() = h_mc, ma->halfedge() = h_ma, and md->halfedge() = h_md. The new vertex uses h01, which points from \(m\) toward \(b\), as its representative halfedge. Since h01 originally started at \(c\), I redirect c->halfedge() to h10 when necessary.

For Loop subdivision bookkeeping, I mark m as new. The two segments of the original edge, represented by e0 and mc, are marked old, while the new cross edges ma and md are marked new. My main debugging strategy was to separate the operation into three phases: record the original neighborhood, allocate all new elements, and only then assign every iterator in the affected region. Explicitly resetting all five iterators of each local halfedge helped avoid stale references and made repeated split and flip tests reliable.

Extra Credit: Boundary edge split

For a boundary edge, only one adjacent face is an interior triangle. I first normalize the orientation of the input edge: if e0->halfedge() is a boundary halfedge, I replace it with its twin. As a result, h00 always belongs to the interior face f0, while h01 = h00->twin() belongs to boundaryface. I save h11 = h01->next() before changing any iterators because it is the first halfedge in the remainder of the existing boundary loop.

I insert m at the midpoint of \(b\) and \(c\). Compared with an interior split, this case creates only two edges, mc and ma; four halfedges, h_mc, h_mb, h_ma, and h_am; and one new interior face f2. The original edge e0 is reused for \((b,m)\). The interior face and boundary loop are then rebuilt as follows:

Region Vertex cycle next sequence Representative
f0 (reused) \(b\rightarrow m\rightarrow a\) h00 → h_ma → h20 → h00 f0->halfedge() = h00
f2 (new) \(m\rightarrow c\rightarrow a\) h_mc → h10 → h_am → h_mc f2->halfedge() = h_mc
boundaryface (reused) \(c\rightarrow m\rightarrow b\rightarrow\cdots\) h01 → h_mb → h11 → ... boundaryface->halfedge() = h01

The new twin pairs are (h00, h_mb), (h_mc, h01), and (h_ma, h_am). In particular, inserting h_mb between h01 and the saved h11 preserves the original boundary loop. I set e0->halfedge() = h00, mc->halfedge() = h_mc, and ma->halfedge() = h_ma. The new vertex uses h_mb as its representative halfedge, so it points along the split boundary edge rather than the new cross edge.

For Loop subdivision bookkeeping, m and ma are marked new. The two boundary segments e0 and mc are marked old because they are the two parts of the original edge.

Part 6: Loop subdivision for mesh upsampling

My Loop subdivision implementation separates geometry calculations from topology changes. All new positions are computed from the original mesh and stored in newPosition before any edge is split. This prevents vertices created or moved earlier in the process from affecting the weights used later in the same subdivision step.

1. Compute updated positions for the original vertices

I first mark every original vertex with isNew = false. For each vertex \(v\), I traverse its one-ring neighborhood with h = h->twin()->next(), count its degree \(n\), and sum the positions of its neighbors. For an interior vertex, I set \(u=3/16\) when \(n=3\), and \(u=3/(8n)\) otherwise. I then compute

\[ v_{\text{new}}=(1-nu)v+u\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_i. \]

The result is stored in Vertex::newPosition; the current position is not changed yet.

2. Compute the position of the new vertex on each original edge

For an interior edge with endpoints \(A\) and \(B\) and opposite vertices \(C\) and \(D\), I store

\[ e_{\text{new}}=\frac{3}{8}(A+B)+\frac{1}{8}(C+D) \]

in Edge::newPosition. While performing this pass, I mark every original edge as old and save its iterator in originalEdges.

3. Split only the original edges

I iterate over originalEdges rather than the live mesh edge list. This is necessary because splitEdge creates additional edges; if those edges were also visited, the loop would continue splitting its own results. Before each split, I copy the edge's saved newPosition. The vertex returned by mesh.splitEdge(e) is marked new, and the copied position is stored in its newPosition.

4. Flip the new edges that connect old and new vertices

After all original edges have been split, I iterate over the updated edge list. I skip every edge whose isNew flag is false. For each new edge, I compare the isNew flags of its two endpoints and flip the edge exactly when one endpoint is old and the other is new. Together with the splits, these flips replace every original triangle with four smaller triangles having the connectivity required by Loop subdivision.

5. Commit the new geometry

Finally, after all topology operations are complete, I copy each vertex's newPosition into position. Delaying this assignment until the end ensures that every weight in the current iteration was computed from the same original geometry.

Subdivision results

Unlike area-weighted vertex normals, which only change the lighting, Loop subdivision makes the object smooth by changing the mesh itself. Each subdivision step splits edges, increases the number of triangles, changes the mesh connectivity, and moves vertices to their weighted positions. On the teapot, the initially visible facets become progressively less pronounced, while the body, handle, and spout develop smoother geometry and silhouettes.

Behavior of sharp features and the cube

Loop subdivision is an approximating scheme, so the original vertices generally move and sharp corners and edges become rounded. Adding support edges by pre-splitting edges near a sharp feature gives the subdivision more control points close to that feature and reduces this rounding.

The cube also becomes slightly asymmetric after repeated subdivision. Each quadrilateral face is represented by two triangles, and the chosen face diagonals do not give all cube corners identical one-ring neighborhoods. Because Loop weights depend on vertex degree and neighboring positions, this asymmetric triangulation produces unequal updates even though the original cube geometry is symmetric. To balance the triangulation, I split the diagonal edge on each of the cube's six faces. This creates one center vertex and four triangles on every face, giving corresponding cube corners the same degree and one-ring structure.

Cube after symmetric pre-splitting

After the six face diagonals are split, each original cube corner is connected to its three adjacent corners and the centers of its three incident faces. Therefore, all eight original corners have degree six and receive equivalent Loop subdivision weights. Compared with the unprocessed cube above, the pre-split cube becomes round in a much more symmetric way and develops a more uniform triangle distribution.

Extra Credit: \(\sqrt{3}\) subdivision

I also implemented \(\sqrt{3}\) subdivision as an alternative smoothing scheme for closed triangle meshes. Unlike Loop subdivision, which inserts a new vertex on every original edge and divides each triangle into four, \(\sqrt{3}\) subdivision inserts one new vertex at the center of every original face, divides the face into three triangles, and then flips all original interior edges. As a result, the number of triangles grows by a factor of three per iteration rather than four.

1. Relax the original vertices

I first mark every original vertex with isNew = false. For an original vertex \(v\) of degree \(n\), I traverse its one-ring neighborhood with h = h->twin()->next() and sum the positions of its neighbors. I compute the valence-dependent weight

\[ \alpha_n=\frac{4-2\cos(2\pi/n)}{9}, \]

and store the relaxed position

\[ v_{\text{new}}=(1-\alpha_n)v+ \frac{\alpha_n}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_i \]
2. Save the original topology

Before creating any new elements, I save every original edge in originalEdges and every original face in originalFaces. These saved iterators ensure that the current iteration processes only the input mesh, rather than recursively processing faces and edges that it creates.

3. Split each original face at its centroid

For each non-boundary triangular face, I obtain its vertices v0, v1, and v2 from h0, h1, and h2. The new vertex vc is placed at the face centroid:

\[ v_c=\frac{v_0+v_1+v_2}{3}. \]

I mark vc as new, connect it to all three original vertices, and create three edges, six halfedges, and two additional faces. The original face is reused as f0, while f1 and f2 are new. I rebuild the three face cycles as follows:

Face Vertex cycle Halfedge cycle Representative
f0 (reused) v0 → v1 → vc h0 → in1 → out0 → h0 f0->halfedge() = h0
f1 (new) v1 → v2 → vc h1 → in2 → out1 → h1 f1->halfedge() = h1
f2 (new) v2 → v0 → vc h2 → in0 → out2 → h2 f2->halfedge() = h2

The new twin pairs are (in0, out0), (in1, out1), and (in2, out2). I also assign representative halfedges to all four vertices and the three new edges. This turns one original triangle into three triangles that share vc.

4. Flip every original interior edge

After all faces have been split, I iterate over originalEdges and flip every non-boundary edge. After the face-split step, each original edge still connects its two original vertices. Flipping that edge replaces it with an edge connecting the two new centroid vertices on its opposite sides. This completes the Root-3 topology.

5. Commit the relaxed old-vertex positions

Finally, I copy newPosition into position only for vertices whose isNew flag is false. The new centroid vertices keep the positions assigned during the face-split stage. Two iterations give a one-to-nine refinement of each original triangle, compared with the one-to-sixteen refinement produced by two Loop subdivision steps.

\(\sqrt{3}\) subdivision results

Compared with regular Loop subdivision, Root-3 subdivision increases the number of triangles by a factor of three per step rather than four. Therefore, after the same number of iterations, the Root-3 mesh remains less dense and its smoothing progresses more gradually, while Loop subdivision refines and rounds the model more quickly. The two methods also produce different connectivity: Loop inserts new vertices on the original edges, whereas Root-3 inserts vertices at face centroids and flips the original edges.

Extra Credit: Boundary support in Loop subdivision

I extended Loop subdivision to meshes with boundaries by using separate position rules for boundary vertices and boundary edges. For a boundary vertex \(v\), I sum only its two boundary neighbors \(v_{b0}\) and \(v_{b1}\) and compute

\[ v_{\text{new}}=\frac{3}{4}v+ \frac{1}{8}\left(v_{b0}+v_{b1}\right). \]

For a boundary edge with endpoints \(A\) and \(B\), the new vertex is placed at the midpoint, \(e_{\text{new}}=(A+B)/2\), rather than using the four-point interior-edge rule. During the split stage, these edges are handled by my boundary-aware splitEdge implementation from Part 5. The remaining stages use the same isNew bookkeeping and delayed position update as the interior algorithm.
